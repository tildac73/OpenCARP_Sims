# Methodology

## Overview and Study Design

The clinical study this work examines estimated the effective field of view of HD Grid electrodes from the decay of voltage and peak frequency across a single-ring isolation line, using Bayesian piecewise regression to locate the distance at which each profile reaches a plateau (Wang et al., 2026). That estimate cannot be compared with the tissue that actually generated each electrogram, because the contributing tissue is not observable in vivo. We therefore reconstruct the clinical experiment in silico, in a model where the contribution of every tissue element to every electrogram is known, and pass the simulated recordings through the retained clinical analysis code. The comparison of interest is between the change point returned by the clinical method and the spatial distribution of contributing tissue calculated directly from the model.

The methodology was developed in two stages. A two-dimensional sheet model was used first to build and test the analysis pipeline at low cost. The final experiments use a three-dimensional, full-thickness slab of atrial tissue in a blood bath, solved with openCARP (Plank et al., 2021). Each modelling choice below is stated with its reasoning and, where the choice is numerical, with the measurement that supports it. Three kinds of evidence are kept apart throughout: values taken from the literature, numerical verification that the implementation solves the stated equations to a stated accuracy, and physiological validation, which this study does not claim. The supporting numbers come from a verification harness kept with the model code (verification/, Table 5); each number is traceable to a result file that records the code revision and software versions that produced it.

## Preliminary Two-Dimensional Model

An inexpensive two-dimensional sheet model was used to develop the electrogram analysis pipeline, the figure generation and the integration of the clinical voltage-decay workflow before any three-dimensional simulation was run. The sheet was isotropic, which removed fibre architecture as a variable and gave a setting in which the clinical assumption of an isotropic hemispherical field of view could be examined directly, acknowledging that isotropy alters electrogram amplitude and width (Leenknegt et al., 2023). A single Courtemanche cell was pre-paced for 20 beats at a cycle length of 1,000 ms and its final state applied uniformly. Extracellular potentials were calculated with a current-dipole approximation in an unbounded homogeneous medium (Jacquemet et al., 2003), with a conductivity of 0.7 S/m (Ištuk et al., 2021); the scar was represented as passive tissue with unchanged conductivity; and activation directions of 0, 45 and 90 degrees were assigned at random between simulated patients.

The sheet model is not used for any result reported in this thesis, for three reasons that follow from its construction. It has no transmural dimension, so wall thickness enters only as an amplitude factor and contributions through the wall cannot be characterised (Aronis et al., 2019). Its forward model ignores the conductivity contrast between tissue and blood. And its passive scar acts as a current sink rather than as electrically isolated tissue. The three-dimensional model addresses each of these. The sheet model's role was to expose pipeline faults cheaply before the three-dimensional pilot, in which, for example, a measurement window containing the pacing stimulus was found to produce a false plateau in the bipolar profile beyond approximately 8 mm, which led to the activation-referenced windows described below.

## Model Class and Tissue Geometry

### Choice of a full-thickness slab

Three geometrical representations of the atria are in use: surface models, bilayer models and full-thickness volumetric models (Aronis et al., 2019). Surface models are inexpensive, can be built directly from electro-anatomical maps, and suit questions about surface activation patterns (Shim et al., 2017), but they contain no tissue through the wall. Bilayer models represent the wall as two coupled surfaces and capture fibre direction and discrete transmural coupling at moderate cost (Labarthe et al., 2014; Aronis et al., 2019), but the myocardium between the layers is not resolved, so the depth distribution of contributing tissue cannot be calculated. Because the first research question concerns the volume of tissue contributing to an electrogram, including its extent through the wall, only a volumetric model can answer it. Volumetric models have been used for the same reason in studies of wall thickness and driver dynamics (Roy et al., 2018) and of electrogram simulation (Martinez-Mateu et al., 2018).

A rectangular slab was chosen over patient-specific anatomy so that wall thickness, fibre architecture, electrode-tissue distance and wave direction could each be varied alone, which curved anatomy does not allow. The same approach was taken by Leenknegt et al. (2023) to isolate the effects of electrode orientation, wall thickness and fibre direction on intracardiac electrograms. The cost of this choice is stated as a limitation: the slab is flat, and no curvature, trabeculation or regional structure is represented.

### Slab dimensions

The slab measures 40 mm along the reference direction of propagation (x), 60 mm across the lesion boundary (y), and one wall thickness (z). The lateral dimensions were set by the requirement that every recording used in the clinical analysis sits further from a slab edge than the largest field of view the clinical study reported. Wang et al. analysed points from 10 mm on the healthy side of the scar boundary to 15 mm inside the scar, and their largest estimate was 13.2 mm (unipolar, roof). With the catheter travel described below, every electrode within that region of interest lies at least 15.0 mm from the slab edge on the lesion side, 20.0 mm from the edge on the viable side and 13.5 mm from the edges in x; the smallest margin is 1.02 times the largest clinical estimate (verification/local_checks.py, geometry). This criterion is necessary but not sufficient: it does not show that the contributions are insensitive to the slab edges, which is tested separately in the Volume Conductor section.

### Wall thickness

The baseline wall is 1.9 mm, the mean left atrial wall thickness of 1.89 ± 0.48 mm measured by computed tomography, excluding fat, at 12 atrial sites in 60 patients (Beinart et al., 2011), rounded to the 0.1 mm transmural mesh spacing. A left atrial mean was chosen because the study models a single, generic slab of atrial wall and not a particular region; no regional scenario is simulated, and the region code under which the data are passed to the clinical analysis script is a dataset label only. The same study reports thicknesses from 0.5 to 3.5 mm across sites, and the sensitivity analysis varies the wall from 1.0 to 3.0 mm to cover most of that range. The two regions of the clinical study fall inside this sweep: region-specific computed tomography in 193 subjects gives medians of 1.14 mm for the inferior posterior wall and 1.55 mm for the roof (Whitaker et al., 2021), close to the 1.0 and 1.5 mm variants. We note that these values contradict the regional argument in the clinical paper's discussion, where the inferior wall is taken to be the thicker of the two regions. The regional differences reported clinically are not reproduced as a paired comparison here; the sweep instead shows how the inferred field of view responds to wall thickness and to fibre architecture separately, against which the clinical regional pattern can be read.

### Spatial discretisation

The slab is discretised with tetrahedral elements of 0.25 mm edge length in-plane and 0.1 mm through the wall, giving 19 elements (20 node layers) through the 1.9 mm baseline wall, 776,020 tissue nodes, and 3,953,670 nodes when the bath is included. Unequal spacings were chosen because the two directions serve different purposes. Through the wall, the spacing sets the resolution of the depth profile of contributions, the quantity the study exists to measure, and the thinnest wall in the sweep (1.0 mm, 10 elements) must still contain enough layers to resolve each fibre layer. In-plane, the spacing must resolve the depolarisation wavefront. An isotropic 0.1 mm mesh would have required tens of millions of nodes (40.7 million was estimated for a 1.1 mm wall) and was rejected on cost.

The adequacy of the mesh was assessed in the manner of the N-version benchmark of Niederer et al. (2011), who showed that conduction velocity in finite-element monodomain codes depends on mesh spacing at 0.5 mm and converges towards 0.1 mm. The tuning strip was solved at the production value of β with only the mesh spacing changed (verification/opencarp_checks.py, spatialConvergence; Table 6). Along fibres the conduction velocity was 0.836, 0.9015, 0.924 and 0.930 m/s at in-plane spacings of 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 and 0.0625 mm, with an observed order of convergence of 2.00 and a Richardson-extrapolated value of 0.932 m/s; the production mesh is therefore 3.3% slow along fibres, against 10.3% for the 0.5 mm mesh of the pilot. Across fibres, where the wavefront is narrower, the velocities were 0.444, 0.530, 0.563 and 0.574 m/s (observed order 1.68, extrapolated 0.578 m/s), and the production mesh is 8.3% slow. Halving the transmural spacing from 0.1 to 0.05 mm changed the velocity of a wave travelling along the strip by 0.21%; this series did not reach an asymptotic order and no extrapolated value is claimed from it, and because the wave travels along the strip it does not test conduction through the wall across the fibre transition.

Two consequences are drawn. First, the along-fibre error is removed from the reported conduction velocity by tuning at the production spacing (see Conduction Velocity), a standard practice which nevertheless means that the tuned parameter absorbs a discretisation error and is specific to this mesh. Second, tuning corrects one direction only. The ratio of transverse to longitudinal velocity is 0.588 on the production mesh, against 0.620 for the extrapolated values and 0.627 predicted for a continuum from the conductivities, so transverse conduction in the model is approximately 5% slower relative to longitudinal conduction than the conductivities imply. This is a known bias of the production mesh, of a size comparable to the sensitivity to wall thickness or fibre model that the study sets out to detect in conduction, and it is carried as a limitation; a 0.125 mm in-plane mesh would reduce it to under 2% at roughly four times the node count.

## Cellular Electrophysiology

### Ionic model

The two long-standing models of the human atrial action potential are those of Courtemanche et al. (1998) and Nygren et al. (1998), with later refinements by Maleckar et al. (2008) and Koivumäki et al. (2011), compared systematically by Wilhelms et al. (2013). We use the Courtemanche-Ramirez-Nattel (CRN) model, unmodified, as implemented in openCARP. The reasoning is specific to the outcomes of this study. The electrogram during activation is generated by the spatial gradient of transmembrane voltage at the depolarisation wavefront; the outcomes are spatial (which tissue contributes) rather than ionic. What the cell model must supply is therefore a reproducible upstroke and a repolarisation that completes within the pacing cycle. The CRN model is the most widely used in the atrial modelling literature, which aids comparison with other simulated mapping studies, and its action potential amplitude (110 mV) and upstroke velocity (187 V/s at a 1 s cycle length) are representative of the human atrial models compared by Wilhelms et al. (2013). Its known weakness is relevant to our protocol and is stated here: under sustained pacing the model does not settle quickly, and in the benchmark of Wilhelms et al. its action potential duration at 90% repolarisation fell to 83% of its initial value over 16 minutes of pacing at 1 Hz, with amplitude still changing after 20 minutes. Our conditioning protocol must therefore be shown to leave a drift that is small against the quantities measured, which is the purpose of the conditioning test below. The Koivumäki model, used with a chronic atrial fibrillation parameter set in a recent simulated mapping study (Rodrigo et al., 2026b), is the natural alternative; it is not included in the current sweep, and the dependence of the results on the ionic model is therefore untested.

### Conditioning and pacing

A single cell is paced for 1,000 beats at the tissue cycle length with openCARP's bench tool, and its final state initialises every tissue node, following the single-cell limit-cycle workflow documented for openCARP (Campos, n.d.). Each tissue simulation then delivers four paced beats and the fourth is analysed. The duration of conditioning, 500 s of paced activity, is shorter than the time over which the CRN model has been reported to drift (Wilhelms et al., 2013), so the state reached is a conditioned state and is not claimed to be a true limit cycle. Initialising all nodes with one state also ignores the dependence of the steady state on local activation time, which the tissue beats are intended to absorb. Stability is tested in every run rather than assumed: between the last two beats, activation times on the catheter-facing surface must change by no more than 2 ms, action potential amplitude by no more than 5%, and action potential duration at 90% repolarisation by no more than 5 ms. These are project engineering criteria, not biological limits.

The conditioning protocol was tested by varying the number of single-cell beats from 1 to 2,000 and measuring the fourth tissue beat on the tuning strip (verification/opencarp_checks.py, conditioning). With 1,000 conditioning beats, the fourth beat had a resting potential of −79.0 mV, an action potential duration at 90% repolarisation of 300.5 ms and an amplitude of 82.2 mV. Between 20 and 2,000 conditioning beats the duration stayed within 2.0 ms, the resting potential within 0.55 mV and the conduction velocity within 0.22%, whereas a single conditioning beat gave a duration 6.3 ms shorter than 2,000 beats. Neither quantity varied monotonically with the number of conditioning beats, so 2,000 beats is a longer conditioning and not a converged reference, consistent with the slow drift of this model. Across eight tissue beats after the configured conditioning, the duration fell from 308.4 ms on the first beat to 300.5 ms on the fourth and 298.9 ms on the eighth, still changing by 0.3 to 0.4 ms per beat, while conduction velocity varied by less than 0.1% from the second beat onwards. The analysed fourth beat is therefore steady in conduction velocity, on which the activation electrogram depends, and within 2 ms of the eighth beat in action potential duration, which is well inside the 5 ms criterion but is not a steady state. The amplitude of 82 mV at a cycle length of 500 ms is lower than the 110 mV reported for this model at 1,000 ms (Wilhelms et al., 2013); the cause was not investigated, and it is a further reason to replace the assumed cycle length with the clinical value.

The pacing cycle length is 500 ms. The clinical study mapped during distal coronary sinus pacing but does not report the cycle length, so this value is an assumption, chosen as a typical pacing rate at which the CRN action potential repolarises fully before the next stimulus. It should be replaced by the value in the clinical procedure records when that is obtained. The stimulus is a transmembrane current of 50 µA/cm² for 2 ms applied to viable tissue nodes within 0.5 mm of the edge from which the wave is launched. Capture is checked in every run. On the tuning strip the capture threshold for a 2 ms stimulus on this band lay between 23.8 and 24.2 µA/cm² (bisection, verification/opencarp_checks.py, stimulusThreshold), so the configured stimulus is 2.1 times threshold; capture was all-or-none.

## Tissue Propagation

### Monodomain formulation

Tissue-level models of cardiac electrophysiology are of two kinds (Clayton et al., 2011). The bidomain model represents overlapping intracellular and extracellular conducting domains and solves for the extracellular potential together with the transmembrane voltage, at the cost of an elliptic solve over tissue and bath at every time step (Bishop and Plank, 2011). The monodomain model reduces this to a single reaction-diffusion equation, and is exact when the two domains have equal anisotropy ratios and no current is injected into the extracellular space (Clayton et al., 2011; Potse et al., 2006). Propagation in the final model is monodomain, with the conductivity along each axis given by the harmonic combination

$$ σ_m = σ_i σ_e/(σ_i + σ_e)

and the extracellular potential is computed afterwards from the saved transmembrane voltage (see Volume Conductor and Forward Model). Potse et al. (2006) found that extracellular potentials computed from a monodomain solution with a separate forward calculation were almost indistinguishable from those of the bidomain model in the whole heart.

The choice was made on cost and its consequences were measured rather than assumed. On a test slab, the same model solved as a full bidomain took 63 and 75 times as long as the monodomain solve for the intact and lesion conditions (4,318 and 4,975 s against 69 and 66 s for 1,500 ms of activity, two MPI processes), because the bidomain solves an elliptic system at every one of the 100,000 time steps per condition. Extrapolated to the production mesh this is 8 to 16 hours per condition on 24 processes, against which a sweep of several dozen conditions is not feasible. The comparison also measured what the monodomain model omits. The forward calculation itself was confirmed: openCARP's own bidomain extracellular potential at the electrodes equalled the lead-field electrogram computed from the same transmembrane voltage to within 4 to 6 parts in 10,000 (median relative difference). Propagation, however, differed: with the baseline conductivities and a blood bath, the bidomain slab conducted 1.67 times faster than the monodomain slab (1.07 against 0.64 m/s on the test mesh). This is bath loading: the blood, which is 1.6 times as conductive as the tissue along fibres and 5.3 times across them, shunts extracellular current near the surface, and in the thin wall of the test slab all of the tissue is near the surface. The effect is expected to depend on wall thickness and was not measured at the 1.9 mm baseline. The measured factor lies within the theoretical bounds for complete shunting, 1.98 along fibres and 1.32 across. With the conduction velocity left unmatched, the clinical change points moved by a median of 1.1 mm (maximum 2.3 mm), and 90% of the bidomain estimates remained within the monodomain 95% credible intervals.

Bath loading is therefore the principal known limitation of the propagation model. It acts mainly through conduction velocity, which in this study is a tuned quantity; how much of the difference in electrograms and change points remains once the two formulations are matched in conduction velocity has not been measured. An augmented monodomain formulation, which approximates bath loading at monodomain cost (Bishop and Plank, 2011), will be adopted only if a production-mesh comparison of the monodomain and bidomain models shows that it is needed. These comparisons used a coarser test mesh (0.5 mm in-plane, 0.25 mm transmural) and three beats, and are not production results.

### Conductivities

No bidomain conductivities measured in human atrial tissue were found. We use the values that Roberts and Scher (1982) fitted to potentials measured in canine left ventricle in situ: intracellular 0.344 S/m along and 0.060 S/m across fibres, extracellular 0.117 and 0.080 S/m, with the two cross-fibre directions equal. These are their reported resistivities (291, 1,677, 852 and 1,247 Ω cm) converted to conductivity, with a rounding difference no greater than 0.0004 S/m. The set was preferred to the openCARP defaults and to the pilot's unsourced values because it is a measured, internally consistent bidomain set with unequal anisotropy ratios, 5.73 intracellular and 1.46 extracellular. Unequal anisotropy matters here specifically. It determines how strongly a wavefront travelling along fibres is weighted against one travelling across them in the extracellular field, and with two fibre layers at right angles that weighting sets the division of an electrogram between the endocardial and epicardial halves of the wall, a primary outcome.

The conductivities are used at their measured magnitudes and are not scaled. This departs from common practice, in which conductivities are scaled until a target conduction velocity is reached, and the reason is again specific to this study. Reaching 0.90 m/s by scaling required a factor of 5.4 at the production mesh, giving an extracellular conductivity along fibres of approximately 0.64 S/m, which approaches that of blood (0.74 S/m). Since the extracellular potential in a bath depends on the ratio of tissue to blood conductivity, scaling would have altered the quantity being measured in order to correct the speed of the wave. The resulting monodomain conductivities are 0.0873 S/m along and 0.0343 S/m across fibres, an anisotropy ratio of 2.55. The use of canine ventricular values for human atrium remains a limitation; canine atrial measurements exist in the secondary literature but could not be obtained in primary form.

### Conduction velocity

The target is a planar conduction velocity of 0.90 m/s along fibres, the left atrial median of the largest human cohort found (89.6 cm/s in 412 patients mapped epicardially in sinus rhythm; van Schie et al., 2021). Other reported central values range from approximately 0.70 to 1.25 m/s, and velocities of 0.70 and 1.25 m/s are included in the sensitivity analysis to bracket them. Clinical values are local estimates that mix directions of propagation relative to the fibres, so assigning the median to the longitudinal direction is a conservative choice. The pilot model's 0.35 m/s lay outside every reported value, and resolving this was the main change in tissue properties between the pilot and the final model.

The target is reached by adjusting the membrane surface-to-volume ratio, β. In the monodomain equation, propagation depends only on the ratio σ_m/(β C_m), so a change in β has exactly the effect on transmembrane voltage of the corresponding change in conductivity; this was confirmed numerically (transmembrane voltages within 4 × 10⁻⁵ mV between the two routes). β does not appear in the equation for the extracellular potential, whereas the conductivities do, so placing the calibration in β leaves every quantity that shapes the electrogram at its measured value. Tuning used a 20 × 1 × 0.5 mm strip with fibres along its length, meshed and solved exactly as the slab, with velocity taken from a least-squares line through last-beat activation times between 5 and 15 mm from the stimulus. Starting from the value tuned at 0.5 mm (0.0218 µm⁻¹, which gave 1.022 m/s on the production mesh), the update β ← β (CV/CV_target)² converged in three iterations to β = 0.027292 µm⁻¹ and a strip velocity of 0.9015 m/s, within the 1% tolerance. This value is one fifth of the openCARP default of 0.14 µm⁻¹ and below physiological estimates. It should be read as a calibration parameter that absorbs the gap between measured conductivities, the CRN model and the clinical conduction velocity at this mesh spacing, and not as a measurement of cell geometry.

The strip velocity is not the velocity of the slab, and the difference was measured for each fibre model on a 16 × 16 mm slab at the production mesh (first beat; verification/opencarp_checks.py, slabVelocity). With the baseline bilayer and the 1.9 mm wall, the surface velocity was 0.791 m/s on the catheter-facing surface, with the far surface activating a median of 1.33 ms later; with a linear rotation of fibres it was 0.770 m/s (1.15 ms), and with uniform fibres 0.875 m/s with no transmural delay. The bilayer velocity depends on wall thickness, because the two layers load each other less as the wall thickens: 0.733 m/s at 1.1 mm, 0.791 m/s at 1.9 mm and 0.834 m/s at 3.0 mm, with transmural delays of 0.52, 1.33 and 2.33 ms. The wall-thickness sweep therefore changes conduction velocity as well as tissue volume, by 14% across its range, and its results are interpreted with the measured velocity of each variant. We retain a single tissue for all variants, so that each sweep changes only its stated factor, and report the measured surface velocity of every run. The baseline surface velocity of 0.79 m/s lies within the range of reported clinical values but below the median from which the target was taken. One discrepancy is recorded without explanation: with uniform fibres along the wave the slab conducted at 0.875 m/s at both wall thicknesses, 3% below the 0.9015 m/s of the tuning strip at the same mesh and β.

### Time integration

The solver uses a time step of 20 µs for both the ionic and the parabolic problem, with Crank-Nicolson time stepping, mass lumping and a conjugate-gradient tolerance of 10⁻⁸. Transmembrane voltage is written every 0.2 ms (5 kHz), which is 10 solver steps and 2.5 times the acquisition rate to which signals are later resampled. A 0.25 ms output step, as in the pilot, is not a whole number of 20 µs steps and was not used. On the tuning strip the conduction velocity was 0.890, 0.9015, 0.907 and 0.910 m/s at time steps of 40, 20, 10 and 5 µs (verification/opencarp_checks.py, temporalConvergence), an observed order of 0.97, that is, first order despite the Crank-Nicolson parabolic step, with an extrapolated value of 0.9135 m/s. The 20 µs step is thus 1.0% slower than 5 µs and 1.3% slower than the extrapolated limit, and its action potential duration is 1.4 ms shorter than at 5 µs. Like the spatial error, this is absorbed along fibres by the tuning of β; halving the time step would double the cost of every simulation for a 0.7% change in velocity, and 20 µs was retained. Each condition comprises 100,000 time steps and 10,001 output frames.

## Fibre Architecture

Fibre architecture determines anisotropic propagation and the distribution of extracellular sources. Diffusion tensor imaging of human atria shows overlapping bundles running in different directions, with a bilayer arrangement of nearly perpendicular layers and an abrupt mid-wall transition in the posterior-inferior left atrium, and a more constant orientation through the wall at the roof (Pashakhanloo et al., 2016). The baseline model follows this qualitative description: fibres lie parallel to the surface, at 0° in one half of the wall and 90° in the other, with an abrupt transition at mid-wall. Although this is conventionally called a bilayer arrangement, both layers are volumetric and contributions are resolved through the whole wall. The angles themselves are assumptions, since the imaging study does not report them numerically. Two alternatives test the dependence on this choice: uniform fibres at 0°, the arrangement described for the roof, and a linear rotation from 0° to 90° through the wall. Conductivities and β are held at their baseline values in each, so that differences are attributable to architecture alone. That openCARP reads the written fibre field as intended was verified by the fall in velocity when fibres are rotated to 90° (0.876 to 0.521 m/s).

## Volume Conductor and Forward Model

### Governing equations

For a given transmembrane voltage V_m, the extracellular potential Φ_e in the myocardium and the potential Φ_B in the bath satisfy

$$ ∇⋅((G_i + G_e) ∇Φ_e) = −∇⋅(G_i ∇V_m)   in the myocardium

$$ ∇⋅(g_B ∇Φ_B) = 0   in the bath

with continuity of potential, Φ_e = Φ_B, and of normal current at the interface between them. G_i and G_e are the intracellular and extracellular conductivity tensors and g_B the bath conductivity. Solving these equations for a transmembrane voltage obtained from the monodomain equation is what is meant here by a forward calculation; it neglects the influence of the bath on propagation, as discussed above.

The source term uses the intracellular tensor G_i, as the bidomain equations require. This is stated explicitly because openCARP's pseudo-bidomain mode, the usual route to extracellular potentials from a monodomain solution (Bishop and Plank, 2011), was found to drive the extracellular potential with the monodomain tensor instead; its potentials agreed with an independent solution using σ_m to within 3 parts in 10⁶ and differed from the full bidomain by a factor of approximately four in amplitude. With the baseline conductivities, the monodomain tensor underweights sources along fibres relative to sources across them by a factor of 2.25, which, with two perpendicular fibre layers, would bias the division of the signal between the two halves of the wall. The pseudo-bidomain route was therefore not used.

### Lead fields

Electrograms are computed with openCARP's lead-field module, which rests on Helmholtz reciprocity (openCARP manual, section 4.2.6). A unit current is injected at the recording electrode and removed at the reference, and the resulting static potential Z, the lead field, is found by a single solve of the volume-conductor problem. The electrogram at that electrode is then

$$ V(t) = ∫_Ω ∇Z(x) ⋅ G_i(x) ∇V_m(x,t) dΩ

where Ω is the myocardium. ∇Z is the sensitivity of the electrode to a current dipole at each point, and G_i ∇V_m is the dipole density generated by the tissue. Two properties of this formulation are the reason it was chosen. First, the volume-conductor problem is solved once per electrode rather than once per output frame, so that electrograms for all 712 distinct electrode positions of a condition are obtained from saved transmembrane voltage, and changes of catheter position or electrode-tissue distance need no new simulation of propagation. Second, the integrand is itself the contribution of each tissue element to the signal, so the same lead fields that produce an electrogram also decompose it. The ground truth is thus not a separate estimate that could disagree with the recording; in every run the electrograms written by openCARP are checked against their reconstruction from the lead fields and the intracellular source (relative difference below 10⁻³).

The module requires openCARP version 20. Its lead fields, the unit-current normalisation that sets every amplitude, and its electrograms are compared with an independent direct solution of the same discrete problem on a test mesh before any production run (acceptance: lead fields within 10⁻⁶, electrograms within 10⁻⁴). This check has been written and has passed against a stand-in solver, but has not yet been executed on openCARP 20, and it is a gate for the production runs.

### Bath, boundaries and reference

The myocardium is coupled to a passive bath representing the blood pool, 5 mm thick above the catheter-facing surface and beside the lateral tissue boundaries, with an isotropic conductivity of 0.74 S/m, the in vivo resistivity of human blood of 135 Ω cm at normal haematocrit (Traugott et al., 1981). No bath lies beneath the epicardial surface, which is therefore insulated; the tissue outside the atrial wall is not blood, no sourced conductivity for it was found, and the alternative of blood on every side is examined as a variant. Homogeneous Neumann conditions are applied on the outer boundary of the bath, so that no current leaves it, and the potential is set to zero on the nodes of a 1 mm cube at one upper corner of the bath, which serves as the reference for unipolar recordings. The bath thickness, the no-flux walls and the corner ground follow Leenknegt et al. (2023), who embedded a slab in 5 mm of bath on all sides with a grounded region of 1 mm³ at a corner.

This configuration is adopted from precedent and was not derived from a convergence study, and an earlier bath study with a different reference (a grounded upper face) did not converge between 10 and 20 mm of bath. We therefore measured how strongly the forward calculation depends on each element of the configuration. Because the bath does not enter the monodomain solution, this could be done without openCARP, on a reduced model that uses the production finite-element operators, conductivities, fibres, electrode definition, filters and contribution definitions, but a coarser mesh (0.5 and 0.25 mm), a 24 × 28 mm slab with a 1.0 mm wall, and an analytic planar wave at 0.9 m/s (verification/forward_sensitivity.py). Four findings follow, and each constrains how the production results are interpreted. First, bipolar signals are converged in bath thickness at 10 mm but not at 5 mm: amplitude fell by 5.2% from 5 to 10 mm and by a further 0.2% to 20 mm, and the radius containing 80% of the contribution mass narrowed from 9.2 to 8.1 and then 7.9 mm. The 5 mm bath of the production model therefore carries a bias of approximately 5% in bipolar amplitude and 1.3 mm in this measure of extent, relative to a bath four times as thick, on the reduced model. Because the bath does not enter the simulation of propagation, repeating the baseline lead fields with a 10 mm bath on the production mesh requires no new simulation of transmembrane voltage, and this repeat is specified as part of the baseline analysis so that the bias is measured, and not inferred, at production resolution. At the 0.25 mm gap the pattern was the same with smaller magnitudes (bipolar amplitude −2.7% at 10 mm and −2.9% at 20 mm; 80% mass radius 7.9, 6.9 and 6.9 mm). Second, unipolar signals did not converge with bath thickness (−16.6% and −19.4%) and depend on where the reference is placed: moving the ground to the opposite corner left the bipolar signal unchanged, as it must, but altered the unipolar waveform completely (relative difference 1.1) and moved its 80% mass radius from 14.6 to 11.4 mm. A unipolar recording in a closed, finite bath is a measurement against a reference inside that bath, and its field of view is a property of the pair. Third, the medium beneath the epicardium is a first-order effect, lowering bipolar amplitude by 30%; it is accordingly a variable in the sensitivity analysis and not a detail. Fourth, extending the slab left the bipolar signal almost unchanged (0.5% in amplitude) but widened the 80% mass radius from 9.2 to 11.4 mm, which bears on the definition of the ground truth and is taken up in that section.

## Lesion and Propagation Protocol

Two conditions are simulated on the same slab, corresponding to the clinical maps acquired before and after single-ring isolation. In the intact condition all tissue is viable. In the lesion condition a transmural strip 5.6 mm wide, from y = −5.6 mm to y = 0, represents the ablation line; tissue beyond the strip remains viable and coupled but is never stimulated, and so is electrically isolated, as the posterior wall is after isolation. This differs from representing the scar as a half-plane of inert tissue: the clinical recordings are taken over isolated but living myocardium, and an isolated region that remains at rest contributes nothing to an electrogram only because its transmembrane voltage has no spatial gradient, which the model reproduces rather than imposes.

The strip width follows the mean endocardial lesion width of 5.6 ± 1.2 mm reported for radiofrequency ablation of the atrial free wall (Friedman et al., 2021), the value on which the clinical study based its definition of the scar boundary. Because 5.6 mm is not a multiple of the 0.25 mm in-plane spacing (22.4 elements), the tagged strip is between 5.5 and 5.75 mm wide; the mesh, not the nominal value, defines the boundary used in analysis. Within the strip the intracellular conductivity is reduced to 10⁻⁶ S/m in every direction, which removes coupling between cells, while the extracellular conductivity keeps its healthy values, so that the lesion remains a passive conductor through which current from neighbouring tissue can flow. [VERIFY: the draft cites a 2016 study by Griener et al. for a lesion model with zero intracellular conductivity and an extracellular conductivity of 0.1 S/m; this reference could not be located and must be confirmed or removed.] The extracellular conductivity of ablated atrial tissue is not known, so two bounding variants are included: one in which the lesion conducts with the sum of the healthy intracellular and extracellular conductivities, a theoretical upper bound, and one in which it is an insulator.

The reference wave travels along +x, parallel to the lesion boundary, so that the intact and lesion conditions differ only in the tissue removed from the source and not in the path of the wave. Because the clinical maps were acquired during distal coronary sinus pacing, in which wavefronts meet the line at various angles, waves at 30°, 60° and 90° towards the lesion are also simulated. In every run it is verified that all viable tissue activates and that neither the strip nor the isolated tissue does.

## Electrode Geometry and Recording Configurations

Electrode geometry and electrode-tissue separation strongly influence the amplitude and frequency content of electrograms (Schlageter et al., 2024). The simulated catheter reproduces the Advisor HD Grid used in the clinical study: 16 electrodes of 1 mm in a 4 × 4 arrangement with 4 mm between centres. Each electrode is a cylinder of 0.5 mm radius and 1 mm length, with its axis along x, represented by the bath mesh nodes inside it. Its potential is the volume-weighted mean of the extracellular potential over those nodes, and its lead field is the response to a unit current distributed over the same nodes, so that reciprocity holds exactly for the discrete problem. This is a passive averaging electrode. Treating the electrode instead as an equipotential conductor changed bipolar and omnipolar amplitudes by approximately 1% and the clinical change points by at most 0.18 mm on a test slab, so the simpler representation was retained.

The two baseline electrode-tissue gaps, 0.25 mm and 1 mm, are the two contact conditions simulated by Rodrigo et al. (2026b) with cylindrical electrodes of the same dimensions. The mesh represents an electrode at the 1 mm gap with 155 nodes in 11 layers. At the nominal 0.25 mm gap the lowest electrode nodes lie 0.3 mm above the tissue, because the gap is not a multiple of the 0.1 mm transmural spacing, and the electrode is represented by 130 nodes (verification/local_checks.py, electrodes). Leenknegt et al. (2023), on a 0.2 mm mesh, reported no results closer than 1 mm because discretisation artefacts affected amplitude at smaller distances. The 1 mm gap is therefore the primary case, and results at the smaller gap are described as an effective gap of 0.3 mm and interpreted with that caution.

The catheter centre is fixed at x = 0 and translated across the lesion from y = −16 to y = 16 mm in 0.5 mm steps, giving 65 placements at each gap, with electrodes from 22 mm on the viable side to 22 mm inside the lesion. Each placement yields 16 unipolar recordings; 9 bipolar recordings, each the larger in peak-to-peak amplitude of the two orthogonal 4 mm pairs sharing a corner, which is how the mapping system treats the HD Grid; and 36 omnipolar recordings from the four right-angled triangular cliques of each of the nine squares, in which the two orthogonal differences are rotated in 2° steps and the direction of largest peak-to-peak amplitude retained. This gives 7,930 recordings per condition and noise realisation, of the same order as the 12,395 clinical mapping points. The omnipolar reconstruction is a search for the direction of maximum voltage and is not the manufacturer's algorithm.

## Measurement Windows and Signal Processing

### Measurement window

The window must contain the local activation at each placement and exclude the pacing stimulus. It is defined before any electrogram is read, from the activation times of the intact simulation: for each placement, from 15 ms before the earliest activation of catheter-facing tissue beneath the grid, but no earlier than 5 ms after the end of the stimulus, to 30 ms after the latest. All placements receive a window of the same length, and the same window is applied to the intact and lesion recordings of a placement, so that the interval observed does not depend on whether local activation survives isolation. For the reference wave, the geometry predicts that activation beneath the grid begins 15.0 to 17.1 ms after the stimulus (for velocities of 0.90 and 0.79 m/s, the strip target and the measured baseline surface velocity) and spans 14.4 to 16.4 ms. Two consequences are stated here and not hidden. The window begins at its lower limit, 5 ms after the stimulus ends, so far-field from the synchronously activated pacing band may enter it; and the wave reaches the far edge of the slab 44 to 51 ms after the stimulus, inside a window that ends at 59 to 64 ms, so wave termination at the edge is also observed. Both are the same in the two conditions, but both could contribute a component that does not decay with distance from the lesion. A slab longer in x would remove them at a cost in nodes; their size is to be reported from the production run.

### Acquisition chain

The 5 kHz unipolar signals are resampled to 2 kHz with a polyphase finite impulse response filter (Virtanen et al., 2020), 2 kHz being the acquisition rate reported for the EnSite X system (Tonko et al., 2024). The resampling filter changes amplitude within the analysed 30 to 500 Hz band by no more than 0.15% and attenuates components that would alias into that band by at least 64 dB; its half-length is 5 ms, and 10 ms of signal is kept beyond each window so that its end effect lies outside (verification/local_checks.py, resampling). Bipolar and omnipolar signals are then band-pass filtered between 30 and 500 Hz, the band reported in the clinical study, with a causal Butterworth filter of order parameter two, which in the design routine used yields a fourth-order band-pass. Its −3 dB points fall at 30.0 and 500.0 Hz and its group delay is 3.8 ms at 50 Hz and 0.6 ms at 200 Hz. The filter type, order and phase of the clinical system are not reported and remain assumptions. Unipolar signals receive the resampling filter only, as no clinical unipolar filter could be established.

The filter is started 200 ms before the earliest window. Its response to a step falls below 0.1% of its peak within 47 ms and no measurable impulse energy lies beyond 200 ms, so the history is more than sufficient; in each run the filtered signals are additionally compared with those obtained from twice the history. A further 150 ms of signal is retained after the latest window for the wavelet transform alone, since a causal filter is unaffected by later samples. The fourth beat leaves 276 ms after the longest predicted window, which accommodates this.

### Measurement noise

Independent Gaussian white noise is added to each electrode signal at the acquisition rate before differencing and filtering, with a baseline standard deviation of 5 µV, three realisations and a noise-free recording, drawn from reproducible streams (base seed 1701, condition and repeat). After the band-pass filter this gives 4.9 µV in a bipolar recording, within the 1.2 to 11 µV that Riccio et al. (2021) measured in clinical bipolar electrograms, although with a different catheter. The level is an assumption and not a measured noise spectrum of the clinical system.

Model amplitudes are reported in the model's own millivolts and are not scaled to clinical voltages. Scaling would import a clinical value into the model against which the clinical method is being compared, and, with noise specified in absolute units, would also set the signal-to-noise ratio. The consequence is that the thresholds inherited from the clinical workflow, which are absolute, sit at a different position relative to the model's signal than relative to a clinical one, and their relation to the noise must be stated. A bipolar recording of noise alone has a median peak-to-peak amplitude of 0.024 mV at the baseline level over a 60 ms window. This exceeds the 0.01 mV exclusion limit of the clinical analysis in every realisation, so recordings consisting only of noise are not excluded, and it lies below the 0.04 mV gate for peak frequency in every realisation. At 10 µV the noise-only amplitude is 0.049 mV and 95% of noise-only recordings pass the peak-frequency gate; at 20 µV and above all do (Table 2). Peak-frequency results at 10 µV and above therefore include values computed from noise, and are interpreted accordingly. For this reason noise is treated as an experimental variable and not as a fixed property: the standard deviation is varied in a doubling series (2.5, 5, 10, 20 and 40 µV), each result is reported against the ratio of noise-only amplitude to healthy bipolar amplitude, and the primary pre-specified test is the displacement of each change point per doubling of noise.

## Electrogram Amplitude and Peak Frequency

Peak-to-peak voltage is the difference between the maximum and minimum potential in the measurement interval. For unipolar recordings the interval is the whole window. For bipolar and omnipolar recordings it is 30 ms either side of the peak-frequency annotation, clipped to the window, following the published description of the clinical system, in which voltage is taken around the peak-frequency annotation and peak frequency is computed only for signals of at least 0.04 mV (Tonko et al., 2024). Where peak frequency is undetectable, voltage is measured over the whole window.

The clinical peak-frequency algorithm is proprietary and is described only as wavelet-derived, so our implementation is an explicit reconstruction and its parameters are modelling choices. We compute a complex Morlet continuous wavelet transform with central frequency parameter ω₀ = 6, a common choice that balances time and frequency resolution (Torrence and Compo, 1998), at frequencies from 30 to 500 Hz in 1 Hz steps, with a normalisation under which the magnitude of the transform of a sinusoid equals its amplitude at every frequency, so that the location of the maximum is not biased towards low frequencies. At each sample the dominant frequency is that of largest magnitude, and samples whose largest magnitude is below 10% of the window maximum are discarded. Two definitions are then carried through the whole analysis: the highest remaining dominant frequency, which follows the published description, and the frequency at the global maximum of the scalogram. Both are retained because, with white noise, the first is drawn towards high frequencies as the signal weakens, opposite to the clinical observation of falling frequency in scar, whereas the second is much less affected. Agreement between them indicates a result that does not depend on this choice.

The implementation was tested on signals of known frequency (verification/local_checks.py, wavelet). Sustained tones at 50, 100, 250 and 450 Hz were recovered exactly. For short bursts the transform reports a higher frequency than the carrier, by 1% for a 12-cycle burst, 2 to 3% for six cycles and 9 to 10% for three cycles, a property of wavelet analysis of broadband transients and not an implementation fault; peak frequency of a brief deflection is therefore a property of the deflection's shape and is not a carrier frequency. The temporal width of the wavelet at the lowest analysed frequency is 31.8 ms, so wavelets centred near the end of a window extend beyond it, and where the signal is truncated the transform suffers the edge effects described by Torrence and Compo (1998). In a synthetic test, a deflection centred 2 ms before the end of the window was assigned 215 Hz with no signal after the window and 152 Hz with 25 ms or more; at 5 ms the values were 164 and 152 Hz, and at 10 ms or more the effect was 2 Hz or less. The 150 ms retained is more than four wavelet widths at 30 Hz (127 ms) and is a numerical margin, not a clinical setting.

## Tissue Contributions and Ground Truth

The ground truth of the study is the contribution of each tissue element to each recording, computed from the same lead fields as the recording. For a recording with fixed electrode weights the signal is the sum of element contributions, φ(t) = Σ_e c_e(t). The transmembrane voltages entering this sum pass through the same resampling and, for differential recordings, the same band-pass filter as the recorded signal; because every operation is linear, the decomposition of the processed signal remains exact.

Two summaries of the element contributions are calculated over the full placement window, and they answer different questions. The signed share,

$$ s_e = Σ_t c_e(t) φ(t) / Σ_t φ(t)²

is the projection of an element's contribution onto the recorded waveform. The shares of a recording sum to one, an identity that is checked to 10⁻⁶ in every run as a test of the implementation and not of the model, and they retain cancellation: a share is negative where an element opposes the recorded waveform. The absolute contribution mass, the time-sum of |c_e(t)| normalised over elements, measures how much each element contributes irrespective of sign. The mass is the primary measure, for a reason found during verification. The signed share divides by the energy of the recorded signal, and when that signal is nearly null, as for a bipole lying parallel to the wavefront, the shares become large terms of opposite sign: on a symmetric test mesh such a bipole gave Σ|s_e| above 10⁴, against 1.03 to 1.15 for unipoles and bipoles aligned with the wave, whereas its mass distribution remained well behaved. Recordings with Σ|s_e| above 2 are flagged and their signed shares are not interpreted. The value 2 is a reporting convention to be checked against the distribution observed in the production run.

Ground-truth summaries are produced from noise-free recordings in both conditions and at both gaps, for every recording at grid centres every 2 mm from −16 to 16 mm (34 placements). The principal comparator for the clinical change point is matched to the clinical experiment, which removes tissue beyond a line and not beyond a radius: for each recording of the intact run, the fraction of its contribution mass that arises from tissue on the viable side of the boundary, as a function of the recording's distance inside the lesion. The distances at which this retained fraction falls to 20% and 10% are reported; the 50% level is uninformative for a wave parallel to the boundary, since symmetry places it at the boundary. Lateral contribution curves at 0.25 mm resolution and depth profiles on the 0.1 mm transmural layers are reported as continuous results. For description, tissue within 4 mm laterally of the recording anchor is labelled near, 4 mm being one electrode spacing and a reporting convention only, and the wall is divided into endocardial and epicardial halves. Element sets containing 50%, 80% and 90% of the contribution are also identified; these are not equivalent to the energy-defined recording antenna of Rodrigo et al. (2026a), and are not presented as such.

Two limitations of these measures were identified by the forward-model tests and qualify every distance derived from them. Unipolar contribution distributions depend on the position of the reference (Table 1), so unipolar ground-truth distances are specific to this model and the comparison with the clinical study rests on bipolar and omnipolar recordings. And because the absolute mass does not cancel, its lateral extent depends on how much tissue the model contains: on the reduced model, extending the slab by 4 mm on every side changed the bipolar signal by 0.5% but moved the radius containing 80% of its contribution mass from 9.2 to 11.4 mm, because tissue far from the electrode contributes individually negligible terms of opposing sign that cancel in the signal and accumulate in the mass. Radii of contribution mass obtained from this model are therefore conditional on the 40 × 60 mm slab, and for this reason the comparison with the clinical change point uses the boundary-matched retained fraction, reported alongside the signed share where that is well conditioned; the dependence of the retained fraction itself on slab size has not been measured and is identified as a required check. The ground truth is therefore exact for the specified discrete model, as intended, but radial extents of contribution mass are not properties of the electrode alone.

## Effective Field of View and Statistical Analysis

The simulated measurements are exported in the structure read by the retained clinical analysis script, which is run unedited after its checksum is verified (Wang, n.d.). Distance is measured from the lesion boundary and is positive inside the lesion; the export subtracts the 3 mm that the script adds when it reconstructs boundary distance from the lesion-centre line, a change of coordinates and not of the lesion. As in the clinical study, the analysis covers 10 mm on the healthy side to 15 mm inside the lesion, excludes measurements at or below 0.01 mV, and trims voltage and peak frequency above their 99.9th percentiles. We note that the supplementary methods of the clinical study describe exclusion below 0.01 mV whereas the script excludes at or below it; the script is followed.

Power-law and exponential decay are compared by the Akaike information criterion on logarithmically transformed outcomes. A simulated slab is a single subject, so the patient-level random intercept of the clinical mixed models cannot be estimated and is replaced by ordinary least squares, which gives the same fixed-effect estimates; the piecewise regression contains no patient term and is unchanged. It fits a linear decline to a change point followed by a plateau of zero slope, on distance for the exponential form and on log distance for the power law, with three chains of 3,000 iterations after 1,500 adaptation iterations, a fixed seed and the default priors of the mcp package (Lindeløv, 2020). Posterior means and 95% highest-density intervals are reported, with the Gelman-Rubin statistic and effective sample size of every fit. One dataset is analysed for each gap, noise realisation and peak-frequency definition.

Before interpreting change points from the model, we established what this estimator returns when the generating process is known (verification/changepoint_recovery.R), using the clinical call and settings on synthetic profiles of 1,500 points over 0 to 15 mm with a decay rate of 0.25 mm⁻¹, the clinical inferior-wall bipolar value (Table 3).

When the estimator's own model generated the data, it recovered the change point without material bias (mean error of 0.03 mm or less at low scatter and 0.28 mm or less at the clinical level of scatter), with 95% intervals of about 0.4 and 1.3 mm respectively; twelve of fifteen intervals at clinical scatter contained the true value, which five datasets per case cannot distinguish from nominal coverage. The other two cases show what a change point means when no break exists. For a signal that decays smoothly onto a constant floor, the fitted change point followed the signal-to-floor ratio: it moved from 8.1 to 13.5 mm as that ratio rose from 5 to 40, with the decay rate and everything else unchanged. It lay close to the distance at which the decaying signal meets the floor at intermediate ratios, 1.7 mm beyond it at the lowest ratio, where the fitted slope was also biased (−0.18 against −0.25 per mm), and short of it at the highest, where that distance approaches the 15 mm limit of the analysed region. The displacement per doubling of the ratio was 1.4 to 2.3 mm, below the 2.8 mm (ln 2/0.25) that a sharp intersection would give. For a decay with no plateau at all, the estimator still returned a change point, 14.1 and 14.6 mm, within 1 mm of the end of the analysed region, with converged chains and 95% intervals as narrow as 0.9 mm. A narrow credible interval and satisfactory convergence diagnostics therefore do not show that a plateau exists, and a change point near 15 mm should be read as the absence of a detected plateau.

Two consequences follow for the present study and bear directly on the interpretation of the clinical estimate. If the plateau in a voltage profile is set by a floor, whether noise or residual far-field signal, the change point measures the ratio of boundary signal to that floor as much as it measures a spatial property of the electrode, and any factor that scales amplitude, including wall thickness, contact and electrode configuration, will move it. This is the hypothesis that the noise doubling series is designed to test in the model, where the floor can be set independently of the tissue.

These properties shape how the simulated change points are used. The comparison with the clinical study is made on the noisy datasets, because the noise-free lesion profile has no plateau: the isolated tissue is silent. Each change point is reported with the relative noise floor at which it was obtained, together with the fitted slope and the logarithmic drop from boundary to plateau. Overlapping catheter placements are spatially correlated and noise realisations are repeated measurements of one slab, not independent samples, so the credible intervals describe uncertainty conditional on the simulated dataset and the model, and not population uncertainty. A change point is compared with the contribution profiles and is not assumed to mark a boundary beyond which tissue cannot contribute.

## Computational Implementation and Reproducibility

Simulations are run on a 24-core workstation (Dell Precision 7960). A pilot at 0.5 mm spacing (88,209 nodes, 384,000 tetrahedra), run on one core for 16 beats of 500 ms at a 20 µs time step (400,000 steps), took approximately 17 hours per condition. Halving the spacing multiplies the node count by approximately 7.5, so the production mesh could not be run serially. Three measures make the programme feasible. Simulations are run in parallel using openCARP's MPI implementation, which partitions the mesh across processes (Plank et al., 2021). On a 16 × 16 × 1.9 mm slab at the production mesh (84,500 tissue nodes, 151 output frames), runs on one and on two processes differed by at most 7.6 × 10⁻⁶ mV in transmembrane voltage and 10⁻⁶ ms in activation time, with the same nodes activating, against project tolerances of 0.1 mV and 0.02 ms (verification/opencarp_checks.py, parallelEquivalence). This was run on openCARP 19.0.0 with two processes; equivalence at 24 processes on the workstation build, and the scaling of run time, remain to be measured there with the same script. Electrograms are computed after the simulation from saved transmembrane voltage, as described above, so that catheter position, electrode size and gap are varied without re-simulation. And the number of paced tissue beats was reduced from 16 to 4 after single-cell conditioning, subject to the stability checks. Lead fields are cached by a fingerprint of everything they depend on (mesh, fibres, conductivities and electrode node set).

Every openCARP setting is held in a single parameter file and every other setting in a single configuration file; each launch writes the fully resolved parameter file it used. Each stage of the pipeline records a fingerprint of its inputs and refuses to reuse outputs produced from different inputs. Software versions, the code revision and random seeds are recorded with the results. The verification results cited in this chapter were produced by the scripts in verification/ and are summarised in Table 5.

## Scope and Limitations

The calculated contributions are the ground truth of the specified discrete model, and the purpose of the study is to relate recording choices to contributing tissue under controlled conditions. Several features limit the transfer of any absolute distance to patients. The geometry is flat. The conductivities are canine and ventricular. The fibre angles are assumed. Propagation is monodomain, without bath loading, and the slab conducts more slowly than the strip on which it was tuned. The bath configuration is adopted from precedent, and Table 1 shows how far amplitudes and contribution extents depend on it. The electrodes are passive, the noise is assumed white and uncorrelated between electrodes, the clinical filter and peak-frequency algorithm are reconstructed from incomplete descriptions, and the amplitudes are not calibrated, so that absolute thresholds sit differently relative to the signal than they do clinically. The pacing cycle length, the clinical sampling rate and the clinical unipolar filter are assumptions that the group's own clinical exports could replace. Agreement between a simulated change point and a clinical value would not by itself establish that the underlying contributions or acquisition mechanisms are equivalent; the comparison is therefore made in order of decreasing robustness: the functional form of the decay, its rate, its response to wall thickness and fibre architecture, and only then the absolute change point.
